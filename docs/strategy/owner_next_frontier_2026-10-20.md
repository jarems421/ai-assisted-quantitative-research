## 3. The main lesson: separate economic discovery from trading certification

This is the most important methodological improvement I want you to consider.

Our earlier workflow often treated initial exploratory research as though it needed the same source and execution certification as a production trading system.

That was disproportionate.

Going forward, distinguish three levels of evidence.

### Level A — Exploratory economic evidence

We investigate whether an unusual measurement is associated with subsequent business outcomes or financial-market behaviour.

This can involve retrospectively reconstructed datasets, provided their limitations are explicit.

Historical data reconstruction, revisions and uncertain availability must be documented honestly.

Such a study may establish an interesting economic relationship, but it does NOT establish that an investor could have traded on the information historically.

Use this level to understand mechanisms and reject weak hypotheses efficiently.

### Level B — Historically actionable predictive evidence

We establish what information was genuinely available to a researcher at each decision date.

Publication timestamps, data versions, security identities and signal construction must prevent look-ahead bias.

Only at this level can we reasonably investigate whether a signal might have been historically actionable.

### Level C — Execution-aware investment evidence

We investigate realistic portfolio construction, fills, spreads, transaction costs, liquidity, slippage, capacity, corporate actions, survivorship and genuine out-of-sample performance.

This is necessary before making strong claims about implementable profitability.

**Do not confuse these levels.**

A Level A result cannot be marketed as historical trading alpha.

Equally, do not prohibit useful Level A research merely because Level C requirements are difficult.

The goal is to apply rigor proportionately, without sacrificing honesty.

## 4. Prioritise naturally timestamped evidence

For our next investigation, strongly favour information whose historical availability can be established directly.

Examples might include:

- Original filings with verifiable acceptance and publication times.
- Publicly timestamped announcements.
- Archived records with stable issue identifiers.
- Immutable or versioned public events.
- Administrative records with independent publication receipts.
- Historical machine-generated observations with verifiable collection and release histories.

Do not assume that every event timestamp equals its publication timestamp.

We need the information to have genuinely existed at the time being investigated.

However, do not turn this preference into an absolute rule.

If a different unconventional dataset is economically compelling but only suitable for a clearly labelled retrospective Level A study, it may still be worth investigating.

**We should prefer information with clean timing, but we should not confuse research accessibility with research value.**

## 6. Discover relationships, not just datasets

I want you to think about the possibility that two individually unremarkable datasets could become valuable when connected.

For example, imagine:

- Dataset A reveals a change in some operational behaviour.
- Dataset B identifies which companies are economically exposed.
- Dataset C provides independently observable business outcomes.

The opportunity might not exist in any one dataset.

It might exist in the relationship between them.

Look for unexpected connections, but demand a credible economic explanation.

Do not create arbitrary combinations merely to generate correlations.

Avoid large-scale feature mining, unlimited hypothesis searches and opaque machine-learning models at this stage.

A single original, falsifiable hypothesis with a clear mechanism is more valuable than 100 speculative signals.

"We built a certified pipeline with 600 passing tests, but no economic hypothesis was evaluated."

Tests matter. But they are not a substitute for discoveries.
