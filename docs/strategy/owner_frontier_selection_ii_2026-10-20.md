## 3. Candidate F02 — Healthcare reimbursement and hidden provider incentives

### Core hypothesis

A medical device may remain clinically covered and commercially available while a change in reimbursement rules makes a complementary procedure less financially attractive to the provider.

This can potentially reduce device utilisation even when the manufacturer's apparent coverage position remains unchanged.

The important information isn't simply whether a device is reimbursed.

It is **whether the provider performing the necessary complementary services actually has an economic incentive to use the device**.

### Investigate the complete chain

CMS rule or coding change → actual payment consequence for a provider → affected clinical workflow → manufacturer-specific procedure exposure → potential change in adoption, revenue expectations or market interpretation.

Investigate original CMS sources, including:

- National Correct Coding Initiative (NCCI) procedure-to-procedure edits.
- Historical effective dates, publication dates and versions.
- Modifier indicators and care settings.
- Relevant professional and facility payment schedules.
- Medicare coverage determinations and associated articles.
- Manufacturer disclosures or billing documentation establishing the necessary complementary procedures.

The existing expanded discovery report identifies Inspire Medical Systems as one illustrative manufacturer, not a preselected trading target.

Do not presume that any edit automatically removes reimbursement.

An edit might be bypassable, inapplicable to the care setting, superseded by another payment rule or irrelevant to actual clinical practice.

**The key measurement question: Can we independently establish an economically meaningful payment difference for a specific required procedure, then connect it to a materially exposed listed company?**

### Challenge the competitive advantage

AAPC, MMIT and other specialised providers already analyse reimbursement rules.

Clinical reimbursement consultants, insurers, providers and the relevant manufacturers may already understand the consequences.

Determine precisely what our interpretation could add.

Would it identify a second-order provider incentive that coverage-focused investors might overlook?

Would the information become available before manufacturer earnings guidance?

Or is this simply an established reimbursement issue already highlighted in company disclosures?

If the proposed advantage is merely reading the same CMS announcement everyone else reads, reject it.

## 4. Candidate F03 — Chemical authorisation and delayed substitution

### Core hypothesis

A regulatory decision allowing an incumbent industrial process to continue may create an unexpected redistribution of economic value.

An incumbent chemical supplier or industrial customer might benefit, while a technically qualified alternative supplier loses an anticipated opportunity for new business.

The key information is not simply whether a substance is restricted.

It is **which specific industrial uses remain permitted, which supply-chain participants benefit, and whose expected substitution revenue is delayed or eliminated**.

### Investigate the complete chain

Original chemical authorisation or review decision → legally permitted use and affected holders → existing customer process → credible qualified alternative → altered economic incentive to switch → supplier-specific financial exposure.

Investigate:

- ECHA applications and authorisation decisions.
- Analyses of alternatives and substitution plans.
- Original decision dates, publication timing and legal effect.
- Specific approved uses and authorisation holders.
- Industrial consortium records.
- Original issuer, subsidiary, customer and product disclosures.
- Evidence that an alternative supplier was technically qualified and commercially positioned to benefit from substitution.

The previous reports mention aerospace and industrial surface-treatment applications. Treat them as possible examples, not a fixed universe.

Do not infer a financially important substitute merely because another chemical or process exists.

A substitute must be technically credible, commercially relevant and connected to an identifiable issuer.

**The key measurement question: Can we identify a documented authorisation decision that changes the commercial opportunity for a specific, financially material alternative supplier—not merely an incumbent chemical user?**

### Challenge the competitive advantage

Compliance specialists and industrial participants may understand these authorisations in detail.

The decision might have been anticipated months earlier.

The substitute's potential revenue might be insignificant relative to its parent company's overall business.

Evaluate whether our inference could meaningfully differ from established regulatory monitoring.

A complex legal interpretation is not automatically a trading advantage.
